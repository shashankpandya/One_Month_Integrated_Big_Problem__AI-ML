Summary

This report follows one bridge through its life. Bridge B33 is a three-span bridge with simply supported spans of 33 m, a 16 m deck carrying three traffic lanes with footpaths and crash barriers on both sides, and six post-tensioned M45 I-girders at 2.70 m under a 225 mm deck slab. Every step from configuration to the maintenance plan was worked out for this one span and is carried forward to the next step, so that the measurements, the synthetic data and the AI/ML results all refer to the same structure.

Three findings carry through the study and are, in my view, the useful part of it. First, the natural frequencies of this bridge change by about 4 % between a cool winter night and a hot afternoon, which is more than most of the deterioration cases change them, so frequencies are only useful after temperature and traffic normalisation. Second, loss of prestress does not change the stiffness of an uncracked bonded girder; it shows in levelling and in vibrating-wire strain long before it shows in vibration or a load test, and the cracking that finally makes it visible starts at roughly 25 % extra loss. Third, the machine-learning models were only as good as the physics in the features given to them: fusing vibration with static, geometric, bearing and NDT data raised the detection quality much more than any choice of algorithm did.

1.1  Assumed site and service conditions

The brief does not fix a site, so I assumed one that makes the later choices concrete. B33 carries a one-way, three-lane carriageway of a divided state highway (one of a pair of parallel bridges) over an irrigation canal and a service road in the coastal plain of West Bengal, about 120 km from the Bay of Bengal. It has three simply supported spans of 33 m on wall-type piers P1 and P2 and abutments A1 and A2; only one span length was selected, as required by §1.2, and span S2 between the piers is the one designed, instrumented and assessed in detail. The climate gives effective bridge temperatures of roughly 15 to 42 °C, heavy monsoon rain from June to September and wet-dry cycles the rest of the year; I therefore took the exposure as severe in the sense of IRC:112 Table 14.1, with 50 mm clear cover to links and 75 mm to ducts. The design life is 100 years. Traffic is mixed, with a high share of loaded three-axle and multi-axle lorries.

Outside the scope are the piers, abutments and foundations (they appear only in the inspection scheme), hydraulics and scour, seismic and wind design, and shop drawings of the prestressing system. All 'measurements' in the report are synthetic; how they were produced, and what that means for the results, is explained in chapters 10 and 11.

2  Bridge configuration

Cross-section. The carriageway is 11.0 m (three lanes of 3.5 m with 0.25 m marginal strips), bounded by 0.50 m New Jersey crash barriers. Outside each barrier there is a 1.75 m footpath, slightly wider than the IRC:5 minimum of 1.5 m because the footpath also carries service ducts for power and optical fibre, and a 0.25 m railing kerb. The overall deck width is therefore 16.0 m (Figure 1). The barrier between footpath and traffic is a deliberate choice: on a highway with fast heavy vehicles it protects pedestrians and it also keeps vehicles off the deck cantilever.

Number and spacing of girders. The construction depth is limited: the deck has to clear the service road below and the approach embankments are already high, so I fixed the precast girder depth at 1.80 m (2.025 m with the slab, span/depth = 16.3). With the depth fixed, the girder number decides how much prestress each girder must carry. Four girders would need about 64 strands each, i.e. six 12T15 cables, which cannot be placed in a 750 mm bottom bulb with the required duct spacing and cover, and the deck slab would have to span 3.0 m clear. Five girders need about 53 strands, five cables, still too many for the bulb at this depth. Six girders need about 45 strands, which four 12T15 cables (48 strands) provide with a small margin. Six girders use about 5 % more strand and 9 % more concrete than four, but they work within the depth limit, give a 225 mm slab with a short 1.7 m clear span and a 1.25 m cantilever, keep each girder under 80 t for lifting by two cranes, and leave more load paths if one girder is damaged. That last point matters for the later chapters: a deck with six girders sheds load from a weakened girder to its neighbours, which keeps the bridge safe but also makes damage harder to see in global measurements.

Girder section. The precast girder (Figure 2) has a 1000 x 175 mm top flange with a 100 mm taper, a 300 mm web and a 750 x 250 mm bottom bulb with a 200 mm taper. The web is 300 mm rather than the common 250 mm because a 92 mm HDPE duct has to pass through it with cover on both sides; the bulb width is set by three ducts side by side with clear spacing larger than one duct diameter. Over 1.5 m at each end the web is widened to the full bulb width (end block) and then tapers back over another 1.5 m, to take the four anchorages and the high shear near the bearings. Section properties are A = 0.855 m², yb = 0.912 m, I = 0.3272 m4 for the precast girder and I = 0.6711 / 0.6790 m4 for the composite outer / inner girder (deck M40 transformed with m = 0.971). The full slab width is effective (IRC:112 cl. 7.6.1.2 gives more than the girder spacing for a 33 m span).

Cross girders. End diaphragms (400 x 1600 mm) are needed to hold the girder ends, to transfer transverse loads to the bearings and, importantly for maintenance, as jacking beams for bearing replacement. For intermediate cross girders I compared one at mid-span with three at the quarter points in the grillage model: the extra two changed the outer girder live-load moment by less than 3 % and the first two natural frequencies by about 1 %, so one 300 x 1450 mm cross girder at mid-span was adopted. It also braces the girders laterally while the deck is cast.

Bearings and joints. Each girder sits on a 400 x 500 mm laminated elastomeric bearing at both ends (five 12 mm internal layers), so the span expands about its centre and both ends move. Strip-seal expansion joints (IRC:SP:69) are provided at each pier and abutment, with a 40 mm gap. The joint at a pier is the place where water reaches the bearings, the girder ends and the anchorages; this becomes the main thread of the year-18 assessment.

Materials. Girders M45 (stressing at 36 MPa, about 10 days), deck slab and cross girders M40, reinforcement Fe500D, strand 15.2 mm 7-wire low-relaxation (IS 14268 Class 2, fpk = 1860 MPa, 140 mm²), corrugated HDPE ducts. Unit weights from IRC:6 Table 1: 25 kN/m³ for concrete and 22 kN/m³ for the 75 mm bituminous wearing coat.

3.2  Structural model

The deck was analysed with a grillage written for this work (Python, about 300 lines): six longitudinal members along the girder lines with the composite section properties, and transverse members every 1.375 m representing deck slab strips (I = b t³/12, J = b t³/6 following Hambly), replaced by T-beams at the cross girders. Each node has a vertical displacement and two rotations. Girder torsion constants are the sum of the rectangle values of flange, web and bulb plus half of the slab. Bearings are vertical springs of 1.6 x 106 kN/m. Loads between girder lines are shared to the four corner nodes, and loads outside the outer girders (barrier, footpath) are applied as a force and a torque on the outer line. For the live load, influence surfaces of moment and shear at 13 sections of each girder were calculated, and every vehicle was moved along the span in 0.25 m steps and across the carriageway in 0.05 m steps; the worst transverse arrangement was found by dynamic programming with the IRC kerb clearances (1.2 m for 70R, 0.15 m for Class A) and 1.2 m between vehicles. The self-weight of girders and wet slab acts on the precast girders individually and was calculated as for a simply supported beam with the 0.45 m overhangs.

The grillage was checked against hand results (Appendix B): with 10 kN/m on each girder the sum of mid-span moments is within 0.2 % of wL²/8 and the inner girder deflection within 1.5 % of 5wL4/384EI plus the bearing compression. For live load, Courbon's method is valid here (span/width = 2.06, stiff cross girders) and gives 2457 kN m for G1 against 2426 kN m from the grillage (Figure 3). For the inner girders the grillage is larger than Courbon, because a vehicle near the centre loads G3 and G4 more than a rigid-deck distribution assumes; I used the grillage values for design.

4  Prestressing system

4.1  Cables and profiles

Each girder has four cables of twelve 15.2 mm strands (12T15, Ap = 1680 mm² per cable) in 92 mm corrugated HDPE ducts, stressed from both ends to 0.75 fpk (1395 MPa, 2344 kN per cable). The jacking stress is kept below both the IRC:112 maximum and the 0.765 fpk used in older practice, to leave room for over-stressing if friction turns out higher than assumed on site.

The layout (Figures 2 and 5) came out of a clash check along the whole girder rather than from the mid-span section alone. At the anchorage face the four anchor plates are stacked in the web plane at 0.35, 0.75, 1.15 and 1.55 m above the soffit, 400 mm apart, which keeps the group centroid (0.95 m) at the end-block centroid (0.93 m). At mid-span three cables lie in the bottom row at 130 mm and one in a second row at 300 mm. The two outer cables of the bottom row must also move sideways by 220 mm, and a lateral swing is impossible inside the 300 mm web. My first layout tried to do it there. In the final layout these two cables drop into the bulb quickly (parabolas ending at 5 m and 8 m from the end) and swing sideways only once they are inside the bulb; the two centre cables follow full-length parabolas and never leave the web plane. The profile of each cable is a parabola from the anchorage with a horizontal tangent where it reaches its mid-span level, straight beyond that point, with a 12 mm allowance for the strands lying low in a draped duct.

Two-stage stressing. Stressing all four cables at 10 days would give a soffit compression of about 18.6 MPa at mid-span against a limit of 0.48 fcj = 17.3 MPa. I therefore stress C2 and C3 at 10 days (enough to lift the girder off the casting bed and carry its own weight) and C1 and C4 at 28 days. Both groups have their centroid at the end-block centroid, so the girder ends are not bent at either stage.

4.3  Stresses at the important stages

Stresses were checked at mid-span, L/4 and two sections near the support for every girder, at five stages: transfer of the first two cables (10 d, self-weight only), all cables stressed (28 d), deck slab cast (60 d), opening to traffic (90 d) and long-term service (t = ∞) in the three combinations. For the service checks the characteristic prestress was used, 0.9 Pm where prestress is favourable and 1.1 Pm where it is not (IRC:112, post-tensioning), and the temperature difference and differential shrinkage were added only where they are adverse. 4.4  Ultimate limit state, shear, deflection and bearings

Flexure. Strain compatibility at mid-span (rectangular block 0.8x at fcd = 0.67 fck/1.5, strands bilinear with fpd = fp0.1k/1.15, pre-strain from Pe) gives a neutral axis depth of 252 mm (block depth 201 mm, inside the 225 mm slab), strand strain 28 x 10-3 (yielded, ductile failure, x/d = 0.14) and MRd = 16 468 kN m against MEd = 12 446 kN m for G1, a utilisation of 0.76. The untensioned bottom bars were ignored, which is conservative.

Shear. The critical section is where the web has just narrowed to 300 mm, 2.75 m from the bearing. With the duct in the web the nominal width is bw - 1.2φ = 190 mm (HDPE ducts). For the worst girder VEd = 1452 kN, the vertical component of prestress (0.9 Pe sinα) is 532 kN, and the net 921 kN is below VRd,max = 1329 kN for cotθ = 2. The required links are 771 mm²/m; I adopted 2-legged 12 mm links at 200 mm (1131 mm²/m) over the end 6 m and 2-legged 10 mm at 250 mm elsewhere (above the minimum of 183 mm²/m).

Deflection and camber. The live-load deflection with impact is 11.8 mm for G1 (span/2786), well within span/800 = 41.2 mm. The camber is 37 mm upward when all cables are stressed and about 11 mm after the deck is cast. The elastic camber due to Pe alone is 57 mm; this number reappears in chapter 10, because a loss of prestress shows up as a loss of this camber.

Bearings. The largest reaction is 1222 kN (inner girder, permanent load 794 kN). For the 400 x 500 mm bearing with five 12 mm layers the shape factor is 9.0 and the total design strain from compression, shear (movement 14 mm from creep, shrinkage and temperature) and rotation is 1.84, below 7 (IRC:83 Part II); the minimum pressure 4.2 MPa is above 3 MPa, so the bearing will not walk. In service each bearing shears by about 0.192 mm per °C (αL/2), which is the quantity the bearing transducers will watch.

Anchorage zone and other details. The end block was sized to the anchor plates (spacing 400 mm, edge distance 250 mm at the top) and to the bursting forces of the local zones; the final anchorage reinforcement has to follow the approval document of the prestressing system actually used. The deck slab was checked separately for 70R wheel loads on its 1.7 m clear span; these local designs are not repeated here because they do not feed the later chapters.

5  Healthy baseline

A healthy baseline is not the design calculation. The as-built bridge is always somewhat different: concrete is stiffer than the code modulus, barriers and footpaths stiffen the deck, bearings are not ideal. What later measurements must be compared with is the bridge as it was measured when it was new, together with a model that reproduces those measurements. I set the baseline up in three steps.

5.1  Commissioning tests

Before opening, a 30-minute ambient vibration test was recorded at night with all 12 accelerometers, and a static load test was carried out with four 300 kN three-axle lorries (two abreast, two in tandem, centred at mid-span) in the spirit of IRC:SP:51. In this synthetic study the 'real' bridge is a model whose stiffness parameters are hidden from the assessment (concrete modulus 6 % above Ecm, crash barriers acting at about one third of their stiffness, dynamic modulus 24 % above static). The commissioning record was identified without using any model shapes, by frequency domain decomposition of the 12 channels (Figure 8): peaks of the first singular value were picked, each peak was named after the design-model mode its singular vector resembled most, and its frequency and damping were refined by fitting a single-degree-of-freedom spectrum around the peak.

I first identified the frequencies by filtering the channels with the design-model mode shapes, which is convenient but wrong at this stage: with the first bending and torsion modes only 5 % apart, the model shapes leaked one mode into the other and gave f2 = 3.78 Hz, while the model-free FDD gave 3.93 Hz. The lesson is that before the model has been checked against the structure, the data have to speak for themselves. Table 8 compares the design model with the measurements.

After updating, the twin reproduces all ten measurements within 0.2 % in frequency and 0.15 mm in deflection, and the measured and twin mode shapes agree with MAC above 0.99 for the first three modes. The second bending mode (14.5 Hz) is almost repeated with the second torsion mode, its measured shape is a mixture of the two (MAC 0.47), and for that mode the twin shape, not the measured one, is used in the spatial filter later.

The permanent system then recorded two 30-minute records a day for a year (02:00 and 14:00), 730 records in all. 9  Vibration measurement and signal processing

Accelerometers. Twelve uniaxial vertical accelerometers are fixed to the soffits of the bottom bulbs (Figure 12): at L/4, L/2 and 3L/4 on both outer girders, at L/2 on the four inner girders, and at L/4 of G3 and 3L/4 of G4. They are low-noise force-balance or piezoelectric seismic sensors (noise about 2 µg/√Hz including cable and digitiser, range ±0.5 g), because night-time ambient response is only 0.5-1 mg RMS and MEMS sensors with 20-25 µg/√Hz would bury the higher modes in noise. Vertical direction only: the modes of interest are vertical bending and torsion; lateral and longitudinal motion of a 33 m span on elastomeric bearings carries little information on the girders.

The layout was not right first time. With the first ten sensors (outer girders at three points, inner girders at mid-span) the AutoMAC matrix of the twin's mode shapes showed two pairs of modes that were indistinguishable at the sensors, MAC = 1.00 (Figure 13): the second bending and second transverse modes both have zero response at mid-span and the same signs at the outer-girder quarter points; the second torsion mode and a higher transverse mode likewise. The shape matrix was singular (condition number of order 1012) and modal filtering impossible. Two sensors at the quarter points of the inner girders G3 and G4 reduce the worst off-diagonal MAC to 0.37 and the condition number to 3.1.

Sampling and duration. The loggers sample at 1 kHz with a hardware anti-alias filter and decimate to 100 Hz, so that modes up to about 20 Hz are well inside the 40 Hz usable band. A record lasts 30 minutes, more than 6000 cycles of the first mode; with 10-minute records the scatter of f1 in my simulations was about ±0.5 %, too large next to the effects to be detected, and 30 minutes with a fitted spectrum brought it to about 0.1 %. Records are taken at 02:00, when traffic is light and the excitation is closest to broadband, and at 14:00 for the traffic response.

Excitation. Ambient excitation is wind, ground micro-tremor and above all traffic. Traffic is not white noise: lorries bounce on their suspensions at about 1.3-2.6 Hz and pass at 40-60 km/h, and their mass adds to the bridge while they are on it. These effects bias the identified frequencies by up to ±0.2 % depending on how busy the bridge is, which is why aRMS enters the normalisation. A drop-weight or shaker test is not needed for routine monitoring but would be used to calibrate damping or mode shapes in a special investigation.

Signal processing. The chain applied to every record is the one in the brief, with two additions:

  • a(t): 12 channels, 30 min at 100 Hz; mean removed, 4th-order Butterworth band-pass 0.5-30 Hz (zero phase);
  • FFT: Welch averaging, Hann window of 8192 points (82 s, Δf = 0.012 Hz), 50 % overlap, about 43 averages;
  • frequency spectrum: auto-spectra of each channel and the full 12 x 12 cross-spectral matrix;
  • modal filter: the channels are combined with the pseudo-inverse of the baseline mode-shape matrix, which separates the closely spaced bending and torsion modes;
  • natural frequencies: a single-degree-of-freedom spectrum is fitted around each peak of the filtered spectrum (frequency and damping), and the mode shape is the singular vector of the cross-spectral matrix at the peak (FDD), compared with the baseline shape by MAC.

Damping estimates from ambient data are poor (scatter of ±25 % in my simulations) and were kept as a feature only to show that they are not useful here.

10.1  What each mechanism does to the measured quantities

Before generating any random data I swept each mechanism from zero to the top of its range on girder G1 (or on all girders where that is the natural case) with the noise switched off. Figure 14 is the most important figure of the monitoring part, because it shows what the measurements can and cannot see before any machine learning is involved.

  • Prestress loss leaves the frequencies and the load-test deflection unchanged until the girders crack, at roughly 25 % loss here; then the load-test deflection jumps (+149 % at 35 %). Levelling (-18.2 mm at 18 % loss) and VW strain (217 microstrain) respond from the start. This agrees with the theory of bonded prestressed beams, where the axial force does not soften the member (Hamed and Frostig, 2006).
  • Bearing restraint raises the frequencies, by up to 2.7 % for f1 when both ends lock, because a horizontal force at the bearing level, below the neutral axis, resists the end rotation. A rise in frequency is therefore not proof of a healthy bridge. Restraint at only one end hardly changes the frequency (the free end takes the movement) but shows clearly in the bearing transducers.
  • Concrete deterioration of a whole girder lowers f1 by up to 5.7 % and raises the load-test deflection by 5 %; a mechanism that changes stiffness over a large volume is the one global vibration sees best.
  • Cracking, corrosion and local stiffness loss change the global quantities by a fraction of a per cent to a few per cent. They are local, and the six-girder deck redistributes load around them.

These changes have to be read against the temperature effect: a 10 °C warmer day lowers f1 by about 1.5 %, which is as much as a 20 % modulus loss of one girder. The brief warns that a change of frequency should not be read as a particular type of damage, and Figure 14 shows why: four mechanisms lower the frequency, one raises it, one leaves it alone, and the environment does both.

12  Feature extraction

A feature should have a physical meaning, respond to at least one mechanism, and be stable on the healthy bridge. This matters because the models are trained on twin data and applied to the real bridge; a 0.5 % error in the twin's absolute frequency would otherwise look like damage.

13  AI/ML-assisted condition assessment

All models were written in numpy and scipy for this work (scikit-learn could not be installed on the computer used, and writing them made the assumptions explicit): a Mahalanobis-distance novelty detector with a shrunk covariance, CART trees and random forests on histogram-binned features, multinomial logistic regression, Gaussian-process regression with one length scale per input, k-means and a Gaussian mixture fitted by EM. The library was split 70/30, stratified by class (2509 training and 1091 test campaigns); every number below is on the test set. The question each model answers is stated with it.

The confusion matrices show what goes wrong. With vibration alone, prestress loss and corrosion are mostly called healthy, and cracking, local stiffness loss and concrete deterioration are confused with each other, because all three lower the frequencies a little and differ mainly in where and how much. 13.3  How much prestress has been lost? (regression with uncertainty)

The size of a prestress loss decides between monitoring and strengthening, so a point estimate without its uncertainty is of little use. A Gaussian-process regression with inputs worst-girder sag, mean level, largest and mean VW strain, load-test deflection change and flexural crack width was trained on campaigns with prestress loss. On the test set it gives a mean absolute error of 2.6 % of Pe (R² = 0.83), and the 95 % band contains the true value in 92 % of cases, so the stated uncertainty is honest (Figure 20). A random forest gives a similar error (2.7 %). A purely physical inverse from the twin's sensitivities (1.04 mm and 12.4 microstrain per 1 % loss) on the worst instrumented girder is worse (MAE 6.4 %), mainly because it cannot see losses in G2 and G5, which have no gauges, and is disturbed by other mechanisms in the two-mechanism cases. The learned length scales tell which inputs the GP relies on: the shortest belong to load-test deflection change (%) and max VW change (ue).

13.4  Where? (localisation)

A random forest on the girder-wise load-test deflections and strains, levels, VW strains and mode-shape asymmetry identifies the affected girder in 70 % of single-mechanism test cases (prestress loss 75 %, concrete deterioration 78 %, flexural cracking 87 %, corrosion 45 %, local stiffness loss 64 %). Corrosion is located worst because its global signature is small and often near a support, where the load test is insensitive; for corrosion the location comes from the NDT grid instead (section 14.3).

13.5  Do the models use the physics? (importance)

The permutation importances (Figure 21) agree with the effect sizes of Figure 15 and with the mechanisms of chapter 10: the prestress model relies on levels and VW strain, the bearing model on the bearing movement ratio, the corrosion model on potentials, resistivity and rust, the concrete model on UPV and rebound. I regard this agreement as a necessary condition for trusting the models; a model that detected corrosion from the damping of mode 1, say, would have learned a quirk of the simulation.

13.6  Limitations

  • All data are synthetic. The scores measure how well the method works if the twin and the noise models are right; on a real bridge they would be lower and would need recalibration with the bridge's own data.
  • The twin is a linear grillage. Bond loss, crack interface behaviour, bearing friction and nonlinear soil-structure effects are represented only through simple rules.
  • Labels are perfect in the library. In practice the label of a past campaign is often unknown, which is why novelty detection on the healthy baseline, not the supervised models, is the first line of the assessment.
  • Six mechanisms were considered. A different mechanism (fatigue of a strand, scour of a pier) would appear as 'novel but unclassified', which is the right output for an unknown situation.

14.3  AI/ML output and the engineer's reading

The fused Mahalanobis distance is 186 against a threshold of 6.9: the bridge is clearly no longer its healthy self (most of the distance comes from inspection features that are always zero on the healthy bridge, such as rust stains). The fused models give the highest probabilities to corrosion (1.00), bearing deterioration (0.95), prestress loss (0.15). The vibration-only models give bearing deterioration 0.84, prestress loss 0.18: they find the bearings, which stiffen the span, and nothing else. The localisation model gives G1 a probability of 0.55.

The prestress estimate needed more care. My first version of the Gaussian process, which took the largest crack width of any kind as an input, gave 18 % for this campaign. It had been misled by the 0.45 mm rust-stained splitting cracks at the joint end: in its training data a visible crack on a girder almost always meant a flexural crack, which appears only after a loss of about 25 %. After splitting the crack feature into flexural cracks and cracks along bars or ducts and retraining, the model gives 9.5 ± 6.3 % (95 % band), and its test error also improved. Because the levels and the VW strain moved mainly in G1, I checked this by inverting the G1 measurements directly with the twin sensitivities (1.04 mm and 12.4 microstrain per 1 % loss), which gives 4.4 ± 3.2 % for G1 (levelling 2.3 %, VW strain 5.8 %). The two estimates overlap; I used the direct G1 estimate for the structural check because it rests on two measurements whose physics is clear, and carried the GPR value as an upper indication.

Is this physically reasonable? I checked it against the mechanisms rather than accepting it. A leaking joint explains the bearing distress at both spans next to it, the wet concrete and low resistivity at the girder ends, and, through the anchorages at that end, a possible loss of prestress in G1; the slight rise of f1 fits restrained bearings; the absence of any change in UPV and rebound rules out a concrete problem; the load test changed little, which is consistent with a girder that has lost some precompression but has not cracked. The ML output is therefore consistent with one cause, the joint, and three consequences. What it cannot tell is whether the strands inside the ducts at the G1 anchorage are corroding; only an intrusive tendon investigation can show that, and the maintenance plan has to include one.

Structural check with the estimated loss. Using the G1 estimate (4.4 %), the soffit margin under the rare combination becomes +0.09 MPa on the design basis (0.9 Pm) and +2.33 MPa with mean prestress, against cracking at -fctm; the quasi-permanent soffit stress is still +4.6 MPa compression. The ULS flexural capacity at mid-span is not affected by a loss of force (the strands yield anyway), MEd/MRd = 0.76. At the corroded B end the vertical prestress component falls, and the net design shear at 2.75 m rises from 690 to 757 kN, still below VRd,max = 1333 kN and covered by the adopted links. The girder is therefore safe today, but it is close to losing its design margin against cracking, and its condition will worsen if water keeps reaching the anchorage.

14.4  Check against the hidden history

The history used to create the data was: joint seal at pier P2 failing in year 9; bearing restraint at end B (stiffness x100) and slight hardening at end A from year 10; corrosion of G1 at the B end, x = 28.5-33.0 m, strand area loss 8.4 % from year 11; extra prestress loss in G1 of 6.3 %. The assessment found all three mechanisms and their common cause, located them correctly, and estimated the prestress loss of G1 within its stated uncertainty. It did not, and could not, measure the strand corrosion inside the ducts; it pointed to where that has to be investigated.

15  Maintenance scheme

The maintenance decision combines all sources, as the brief asks: visual inspection + NDT + structural response + vibration monitoring + AI/ML. In my scheme the ML output is one line of evidence among them. An action is triggered by the evidence itself, a crack, a potential map, a bearing that does not move, and the ML result mainly sets the priority and tells what to check next. A probability of 0.8 for 'prestress loss' is not a reason to strengthen a girder; it is a reason to measure the force.

Sequence and justification. Drains and the joint come first because every other defect here is a consequence of water; repairing concrete or bearings while the joint still leaks would waste the repair. The bearings of S2 end B (and S3 end A, same joint) are replaced by lifting each span on jacks placed under the end diaphragm, which was designed as a jacking beam; the lift is limited to 10 mm and the joint is closed to traffic for one night per span. Concrete repair at the G1 end zone and the pier cap follows the half-cell map, with an anti-carbonation coating on repaired and low-cover areas and a migrating corrosion inhibitor where potentials are in the uncertain band. The tendon investigation (GPR to locate the ducts, impact-echo along them, borescope through a small drilled port at the anchorage and at duct high points) and a lift-off or stress-release test on G1 decide the last step: if the measured force confirms a loss that removes the margin against cracking, external post-tensioning (two straight 12T15 tendons per girder with deviators at the mid-span cross girder and anchors on the end diaphragms) restores it; if not, the girder is kept under closer monitoring.

Inspection and monitoring intervals. Until the works are complete: routine inspection monthly until works, monitoring continuous, weekly review; principal inspection after works, then 1 year. Crack gauges and two extra VW gauges at the G1 end zone and bearing transducers on all girders of S2 are added at once and kept for at least two years after the repair. After the works, the commissioning steps of chapter 5 are repeated (ambient test, load test, NDT of the repaired zones) and become the new baseline; the bridge returns to the routine regime (pre/post-monsoon routine inspections, principal inspection every three years, quarterly review of the monitoring) when two years of data after repair stay inside the new limits.

Longer-term improvement. Each of the four expansion joints is a source of water. When the S2-S3 joint is replaced, it is worth considering a continuity (link) slab over the piers, which would leave only the two abutment joints. The drain spouts of all spans should be extended at least 100 mm below the girder soffit with a drip groove, which costs little and removes the second source of wetting found in this assessment.

16  Use of AI tools and verification

As permitted by §1.15 of the brief, I used an AI assistant (Claude, by Anthropic) throughout: to discuss the problem and the approach, to write and debug most of the Python code, to generate the synthetic data, and to draft and edit parts of this report. The assumptions, the engineering choices and the interpretation are mine, and every result was checked before it was used. The brief says that an AI-generated result is not a verified engineering result; the most useful part of the interaction was the list of things that were wrong at first and were changed after checking:

  • The first cable layout swung two cables sideways inside the 300 mm web, which is impossible; the profiles were redrawn so the lateral swing happens inside the bottom bulb, and a clash check along the whole girder was added.
  • Stressing all four cables at 10 days gave 18.6 MPa at the soffit against a limit of 17.3 MPa; two-stage stressing was adopted.
  • The first vibration model gave peak accelerations of 170 mg because the lorry bounce frequencies overlapped the first mode and the ambient forcing was too strong; both were recalibrated until the night-time RMS (0.6-0.8 mg) and the traffic peaks (10-25 mg) were of the order reported for bridges of this size.
  • With 10-minute records and peak picking the scatter of f1 was ±0.5 %; 30-minute records with a fitted spectrum reduced it to about 0.1 %.
  • The AutoMAC check showed that the first sensor layout could not separate two pairs of modes; two accelerometers were added.
  • Identifying the commissioning test with the design-model shapes biased f2 by 3.6 %; the commissioning test is now identified model-free (FDD).
  • The first synthetic inspection saw every hardened bearing and every 2 % corrosion case, which made inspection look perfect; detection was made to depend on severity, and leaking joints were also allowed on sound bridges.
  • The first prestress-loss model mistook corrosion cracks for flexural cracks and estimated 18 % loss for the year-18 campaign; the crack feature was split by crack type and the result checked against a direct physical inversion (section 14.3).
  • The novelty detector failed at first on the inspection features (singular covariance, because a healthy bridge never has rust stains); a small floor on the covariance was added and its effect on the false-alarm rate checked.
  • The impact factor for 70R wheeled vehicles on a 33 m span, the IRC:6 footway load for spans over 30 m and the three-lane reduction were checked against the code text rather than taken from the assistant's first answer.

Appendix B lists 20 independent checks of the computations by simpler methods (hand formulas, a separate influence-line calculation, Courbon's method, beam-frequency formulas, recovery of the known parameters); 20 of 20 pass their tolerances. The span, the configuration, the prestressing system, the deterioration scenarios and values, the synthetic data and the maintenance scheme were all developed for this report, as the brief requires for an individual problem (§1.16).

17  Conclusions

  • A 33 m span with six 1.80 m post-tensioned M45 girders at 2.70 m, four 12T15 cables each and two-stage stressing satisfies IRC:6 / IRC:112 with the soffit uncracked under the rare combination (margin about 1 MPa) and MEd/MRd = 0.76. The girder number was set by the limited construction depth rather than by material quantity.
  • A healthy baseline must be measured, not calculated. The design model was 7-10 % off in frequency; after updating three parameters on the commissioning tests the twin reproduced them within 0.2 %, and one year of data gave the temperature and traffic normalisation without which frequencies are not usable here (4 % seasonal swing).
  • Vibration monitoring alone is poor at detecting the mechanisms that usually decide the life of a post-tensioned bridge, prestress loss and corrosion. Levelling, embedded VW strain and bearing transducers are cheap and do it much better; NDT and inspection are indispensable for corrosion.
  • Data fusion, not the algorithm, made the AI/ML useful: macro-F1 0.32 with vibration, 0.90 fused; the models' feature importances agree with the physics, and the Gaussian process gives prestress loss with an honest uncertainty.
  • Applied to the bridge at year 18, the assessment traced the defects to one cause (a leaking joint), located them, estimated the prestress loss of the affected girder and produced a maintenance plan that removes the cause, repairs the damage, measures what the monitoring can only infer, and re-baselines the bridge.
